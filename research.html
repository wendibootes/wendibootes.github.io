Research

My scholarship examines the relationship of narrative form to the histories of empire and colonial power. I am guided by the conviction that literature reveals the limits and contingencies of political categories—“nation,” “empire,” “colony”—that continue to shape our world, and that by attending to narrative’s formal choices we can make visible historical transformations that go unmarked in other kinds of discourse. I am a comparatist by disposition and training: my work moves across British, Irish, Russian, and Indian literatures to ask how writers working from different positions within and against imperial power negotiate the formal challenges posed by their historical moment.

Book Project

Unsettling Empire: Modernist Narratives of Imperial Crisis

Unsettling Empire proceeds from a persistent formal paradox: moments of acute historical rupture—revolution, decolonization, imperial collapse—are repeatedly accompanied, in early twentieth-century Irish, Russian, and Anglo-Indian prose, by a striking vagueness of narrative environment. As the British Empire contracted in Ireland and India and the Russian Empire gave way to the Soviet federation, writers confronted a world that was immediate and vivid, yet paradoxically difficult to fully apprehend. The result, across three literary traditions, is what the book calls elusive setting — narrative environments marked by vagueness, unstable naming, perspectival slippage, and distortions of scale that register the perceptual strain of living through imperial dissolution.

The book is organized around a series of formal problems of setting that emerge with force under circumstances of imperial delegitimization. The first section, Insufficiency, turns to the Irish revolutionary period, when the British imperial framework rapidly loses coherence and legitimacy in Ireland. Here, I show how James Joyce’s A Portrait of the Artist as a Young Man and Elizabeth Bowen’s The Last September mark this loss as failures of naming and descriptive precision, respectively. The second, Disorientation, moves to revolutionary Russia, where the problem shifts from the inadequacy of names to the destruction of the perceptual frameworks through which space is organized. Viktor Shklovsky’s A Sentimental Journey approaches the October Revolution as an absence to be circled through detail and digression rather than narrated directly, while Isaac Babel’s Konarmiia (Red Cavalry) locates formal innovation at the arrière-garde, where peripheral voices and warped proportions reveal what the vanguard perspective cannot see. The final section, Longing, follows Indian and Soviet texts suspended between a vanished imperial order and an indeterminate future. These are environments shaped by what has failed or not yet arrived: the utopian void of Andrei Platonov’s Kotlovan (Foundation Pit) and the elegiac repetitions of Ahmed Ali’s and Raja Rao’s late colonial fiction (Twilight in Delhi and Kanthapura). Across these sections, the book charts a shift from the erosion of linguistic clarity to conditions of absence; these narrative settings, with their various formal distortions, constitute a fluctuating medium through which historical transformation is dimly apprehended.

By locating historical knowledge in the background of fiction rather than in its events or arguments, Unsettling Empire offers an alternative account of how novels think about political transformation. Against histories of imperial collapse and decolonization that privilege decisive rupture and revolutionary clarity, the writers gathered here suggest that empire’s end is experienced as something dimmer and more ambient: pressure without clear object, a strangeness in the texture of space itself. In recovering setting as a central category of modernist aesthetics, the book proposes that the vague, the partial, and the indeterminate are not aesthetic deficiencies but the very forms through which a world in dissolution becomes thinkable.

Second Project

A second project examines an uneasy alliance between anti-colonial and socialist imaginaries in interwar global Anglophone fiction. Focusing on figures including Mulk Raj Anand, C.L.R. James, and Claude McKay, it asks how they negotiated competing aesthetic paradigms: Soviet socialist realism, Anglo-Irish modernism, and the European realist tradition. These were writers caught between the overlapping currents of Comintern internationalism, national liberation movements, and Anglo literary hegemony — each making different and often irreconcilable demands on narrative form. The project is interested in what formal innovations emerged from navigating these incompatible demands, and what they reveal about the entanglement of literary form and political imagination in the interwar period.
